# Losing a Developer to Burnout (Leadership Failure and Lesson)

## Purpose

Engineering story documenting the hardest people situation from my Lead Web Developer period at Advision, and the one I handled worst. This is a genuine **failure story**: use it for "biggest failure," "a time you'd do something differently," and "difficult team situation" questions.

This document is honest by design. It should never be edited into a story where I look good. The value is the lesson.

Facts live here; interview answers reference this file, they do not duplicate it.

**Employer:** Advision Development (Lead Web Developer period, 2020). See [`../experience/advision.md`](../experience/advision.md) for role context.

---

## Context

As Lead Web Developer I led a frontend team of 4. One team member was the **most experienced developer on the team after me** (the other two were recent graduates). He had transferred onto our React team from a project widely seen as the least desirable in the company, and he was genuinely capable: he knew React well and delivered his assigned work.

The company culture pushed developers hard and expected them to take on more than what was strictly assigned. The company's Project Manager wanted more from him specifically, because of his seniority relative to the rest of the team: more initiative, more of a senior or lead trajectory.

He did not want that. He wanted to deliver his tickets well and go home. That was his honest, consistent position.

---

## What Happened

Caught between the PM's expectations and his preferences, we kept pushing him toward a bigger role that he had clearly signaled he did not want.

I was new to leading people and, honestly, I did not have the soft skills yet to handle it well. I did not manage the situation correctly: I did not push back effectively on the expectations being placed on him, and I did not protect the arrangement that was actually working (a capable developer reliably delivering his scope).

He disliked the pressure, and from what I could see he **burned out. He eventually quit.** We have not spoken since, which itself says something about how it ended.

The experience soured me on the lead role for a while. It is a large part of why I describe my leadership style today as influence through ownership rather than authority.

---

## What I Got Wrong

- **I treated his lack of ambition as a problem to fix** instead of a legitimate preference to respect. A developer who reliably delivers assigned work is an asset, not an underperformer.
- **I did not shield him from organizational pressure.** A lead's job includes pushing back upward when expectations do not fit the person. I let the pressure flow through me instead of stopping some of it.
- **I confused the org's definition of growth with his.** Not everyone wants the senior/lead track, and pushing it on someone is a reliable way to lose them.
- **I noticed the burnout too late to change course.** The signals were there before he quit.

---

## Lessons Learned

- **Meet people where they are.** Ask what someone wants from their career before assuming the standard ladder applies to them.
- **Retention is a leadership outcome.** Losing a capable, delivering developer is a worse result than having a team member who "only" completes his tickets.
- **A lead absorbs pressure downward selectively.** Passing every stakeholder expectation straight through to the team is not leadership, it is routing.
- **Soft skills are learned skills.** I lacked them then; I have deliberately paid attention to this ever since, and it shapes how I mentor and collaborate today.

---

## Interview Usage

Good for:

- Tell me about your biggest failure
- Tell me about a difficult team member / people situation
- What would you do differently as a leader?
- What did being a lead teach you?

**Suggested framing:** Own the failure plainly (I was a new lead without the soft skills, and I handled it badly), state the lesson, and connect it to how I lead now. Do not blame the developer, and keep the description of company culture factual and measured.

**Avoid:** Framing him as a problem employee; venting about the company; implying the outcome was inevitable.
